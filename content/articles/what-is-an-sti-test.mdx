An STI test checks whether you have a sexually transmitted infection, also called an STD. It usually means giving a urine sample, a swab, or a small blood sample. You can test at a sexual health clinic, through your GP, or with a home kit sent to your door. Many STIs cause no symptoms, so testing is the only way to know for sure.

## What Does "STI Test" Actually Mean?

STI stands for sexually transmitted infection. STD stands for sexually transmitted disease. In everyday language, people use both terms to mean the same thing: a test that checks for an infection passed on through sex.

Doctors and the NHS mostly use "STI" now. That's because you can carry an infection without it ever causing symptoms or turning into a "disease." It's a more accurate word, not a different test. If you see "STD test" somewhere, it means exactly the same as "STI test."

## Why Get Tested?

Getting tested isn't just for when something feels wrong. There are good reasons to test even when you feel completely fine.

- It gives you certainty. You'll know exactly where you stand, instead of guessing.
- Most STIs are easy to treat, especially if you catch them early.
- It protects any partner you have sex with, now or in future.
- Left untreated, some STIs can lead to more serious health problems later on.

Think of it like any other routine health check. It doesn't mean anything has gone wrong. It just means you're looking after yourself.

## Do I Need Symptoms to Get Tested?

No. This is one of the most important things to understand about STIs: most of them don't cause any symptoms, especially early on.

Chlamydia, the most commonly diagnosed STI in the UK, usually causes no symptoms at all. That means someone can have it, pass it on, and have no idea, unless they get tested. If you've had sex without a condom, or you're starting a new relationship, testing is worth doing whether or not you feel any different.

## How Does an STI Test Work?

An STI test uses one of three types of sample, depending on which infections are being checked.

- **Urine sample** — used to check for infections like chlamydia and gonorrhoea.
- **Swab** — a small, soft swab taken from the area that may be affected. It's quick and shouldn't hurt.
- **Blood sample** — used to check for infections like HIV and syphilis.

You don't need every type of sample for every test. It depends on which infections you're checking for.

**Testing at a clinic or through your GP.** A nurse or doctor will talk you through which test is right for you, take the sample, and explain when to expect your results. Some clinics offer certain results, like a rapid HIV test, on the same day.

**Testing with a home kit.** A home test kit lets you do most of this yourself, in private:

- Order a kit (online or from a pharmacy) and it arrives by post.
- Follow the instructions to collect your sample at home: usually a urine sample or a swab.
- Post the sample back using the prepaid packaging provided.
- Get your results online or by text, usually within about a week.

Home kits are a good option if you'd rather not visit a clinic, or if getting to one isn't easy for you.

## How Soon After Sex Should I Test?

This matters more than people expect. Test too early, and an infection may not yet be detectable, even if you have it. This gap is called the "window period."

## How Long Do Results Take?

It depends on where and how you test. Some clinic-based tests, like a rapid HIV test, can give you a result the same day. Tests sent to a lab, including most home kits, usually take around a week.

## What Happens If I Test Positive?

First: it's common, and it's manageable. Most STIs are straightforward to treat, often with a short course of antibiotics or another simple treatment.

If you test positive, you'll be given clear next steps, which might include treatment, a follow-up test to confirm you're clear, and advice on telling any partners who may also need to test. Clinics and the NHS can help you notify partners anonymously if you'd rather not do it yourself.

## Is STI Testing Free in the UK?

Yes. Testing through the NHS, whether at a sexual health clinic or your GP, is free. Many areas also offer free NHS-funded home test kits, particularly for people under 25. Paid options also exist if you'd prefer a specific provider or faster turnaround, but cost should never be a reason to put off testing.

## Are Home Test Kits Accurate?

When used correctly and sent to a lab, home test kits use the same methods as a clinic test, so accuracy is comparable. Following the instructions carefully, especially around timing and sample collection, makes the biggest difference to getting a reliable result. Some rapid, instant-read kits are slightly less sensitive than lab-processed ones, so a positive result from a rapid kit is usually confirmed with a follow-up lab test.

## Which STIs Does a Standard Test Cover?

A standard STI test usually checks for the most common infections: chlamydia, gonorrhoea, HIV, and syphilis. A fuller screen can also include herpes, hepatitis B and C, and other less common infections. If you're not sure which test you need, a clinic, GP, or the provider of a home kit can help you choose the right one for your situation.

## In Short

An STI test is a simple, private way to find out whether you have an infection passed on through sex. You don't need symptoms to need one. Testing usually means a urine sample, a swab, or a blood sample, whether you're at a clinic, at your GP, or testing at home. Most infections are easy to treat, results are usually back within a week, and NHS testing is free. If in doubt, test. It's the only way to know for sure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the difference between an STI and an STD?**

Nothing important. They mean the same thing. "STI" is simply the term most doctors use now.

**Can I get tested if I have no symptoms?**

Yes, and you should. Most STIs, including chlamydia, usually cause no symptoms at all.

**How soon after unprotected sex can I test?**

It depends on the infection: 2 weeks for chlamydia and gonorrhoea, up to 7 weeks for HIV, and 12 weeks for syphilis.

**How often should I get tested?**

If you're sexually active with new or multiple partners, testing once a year is a sensible baseline, and after any time you have sex without a condom with a new partner.

**Do I have to tell my partner if I test positive?**

It's strongly recommended, since they may need testing and treatment too. If you'd rather not do it yourself, clinics and the NHS can help notify partners anonymously on your behalf.

**Can I test while I'm on my period?**

Yes, for most tests. A urine or blood sample isn't affected. For a swab test, mention it when you book or read the kit instructions, as timing can occasionally matter.
